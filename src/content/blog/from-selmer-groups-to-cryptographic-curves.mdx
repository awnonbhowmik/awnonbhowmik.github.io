# From Selmer Groups to Cryptographic Curves: A Reproducible Walkthrough

An elliptic curve used in cryptography begins with public numbers: a finite field, coefficients defining the curve, a base point, a subgroup order, and a cofactor. Those numbers are not secret. Yet their origin matters.

If a standard publishes only the final parameters, an implementer may be able to verify that the curve works without being able to explain why that curve—rather than one of the enormous number of alternatives—was selected. A transparent generation process addresses a different question from ordinary validation:

> Can an independent observer reproduce the path from public inputs to the final curve, including every rejection and retry?

My 2025 preprint, *Selmer-Inspired Elliptic Curve Generation*, explores one possible answer. It uses algebraic forms associated with 2- and 3-descent as structured inputs, derives candidate invariants, filters them using local-solubility proxies, reconciles the results deterministically, and then applies conventional cryptographic checks (Bhowmik, 2025).

This article explains that pipeline for a broader technical audience. It is a walkthrough of an exploratory construction—not a recommendation to replace standardized curves, and not production cryptographic guidance.

---

## 1. Curve Generation Is Not Key Generation

The first distinction is essential.

**Curve generation** selects shared, public domain parameters. For a short-Weierstrass curve over a prime field, a parameter set normally includes

$$
(p,A,B,G,r,h),
$$

where:

- $p$ defines the field $\mathbb{F}_p$;
- $A$ and $B$ define $E: y^2=x^3+Ax+B$;
- $G$ is a public base point;
- $r$ is the prime order of the subgroup generated by $G$; and
- $h=\#E(\mathbb{F}_p)/r$ is the cofactor.

**Key generation** happens later. A user samples a private scalar $d$ and computes a public point $Q=dG$. Generating a new private key does not require inventing a new curve.

This article concerns the first problem: how a community might arrive at the shared public parameters. NIST SP 800-186 specifies recommended elliptic-curve domain parameters for U.S. federal use, including the field, curve representation, subgroup order, cofactor, and base point (National Institute of Standards and Technology [NIST], 2023). The Selmer-inspired proposal is a research direction about parameter provenance; it is not a substitute for those deployment requirements.

---

## 2. Why Provenance Matters

Security validation and provenance answer related but distinct questions.

- **Validation asks:** Is the curve nonsingular? Does it contain a large prime-order subgroup? Is the cofactor acceptable? Does the twist have safe structure? Are anomalous and low-embedding-degree cases excluded?
- **Provenance asks:** What fixed inputs and rules produced this particular candidate? Could a designer have searched privately through many acceptable curves and selected one for an undisclosed reason?

There is no known attack that breaks a sound curve merely because its generation story is incomplete. The concern is instead about trust and the possibility of undisclosed selection criteria. Modern designs have responded in several ways. Brainpool published pseudorandom generation procedures and seeds (Lochter & Merkle, 2010). Curve25519 used explicit performance and security criteria that make its design choices comparatively easy to inspect (Bernstein, 2006). Broader curve-selection research emphasizes that security, efficiency, and implementation behavior must be evaluated together (Bos et al., 2016).

The Selmer-inspired approach adds another option: make the candidate's ancestry a sequence of mathematical objects that can be placed in an audit transcript.

---

## 3. The Arithmetic-Geometry Idea in Plain Language

Elliptic curves belong to two worlds relevant here.

Over a finite field $\mathbb{F}_p$, their points form a finite group. Cryptography uses that group and relies on the difficulty of recovering a scalar $d$ from points $G$ and $dG$—the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem introduced into cryptography independently by Miller (1986) and Koblitz (1987).

Over the rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$, elliptic curves are studied as Diophantine objects: we ask which rational points exist and how those points are organized. **Descent** is a family of methods that replaces a difficult question about rational points with structured auxiliary equations plus local consistency conditions. Selmer's work on cubic Diophantine equations and Cassels's development of the arithmetic of genus-one curves are foundational to this theory (Cassels, 1962; Selmer, 1951).

Very roughly, an $n$-Selmer group records covering objects that satisfy necessary local conditions for coming from rational points. “Local” means examining solvability in completions such as the real numbers and the $p$-adic numbers. A covering may look valid everywhere locally without corresponding to a global rational point; the gap is connected to the Tate–Shafarevich group. Silverman (2009) gives the standard modern development.

For this walkthrough, the critical fact is more concrete:

- 2-descent can be represented using **binary quartic forms**.
- 3-descent can be represented using **ternary cubic forms**.
- Those forms have classical algebraic invariants that can be related to elliptic-curve invariants.

That creates a bridge from arithmetic geometry to parameter generation.

---

## 4. Why the Word “Inspired” Is Doing Important Work

The proposed pipeline does **not** claim that computing a Selmer group automatically produces a secure cryptographic curve. Nor does passing a bounded finite-field solubility test establish membership in an arithmetic Selmer group.

Instead, the construction borrows three design ideas from descent:

1. Begin with structured auxiliary forms rather than choosing $A$ and $B$ directly.
2. Use invariants of those forms to derive candidate curve data.
3. Reject forms that fail explicit solubility-inspired filters.

The proposal then leaves arithmetic geometry and enters conventional cryptographic validation. Group order, twist order, cofactor, anomalous structure, and embedding degree—not the Selmer inspiration—determine whether a candidate survives (Bhowmik, 2025).

This separation prevents a dangerous category error: **interesting provenance is not a security proof**.

## 5. Overview of the Pipeline

At a high level, the construction is a deterministic retry loop:

1. Fix a prime $p$, a domain-separation string $DS$, and a public seed $\sigma$.
2. Derive independently labeled hash streams.
3. Construct a binary quartic and compute its invariants.
4. Apply nondegeneracy and local-solubility-inspired filters.
5. Construct a ternary cubic and compute its invariants.
6. Apply the corresponding filters.
7. Reconcile the two invariant pairs into one candidate.
8. Instantiate an elliptic curve and reject a zero discriminant.
9. Count points and apply cryptographic security filters.
10. If any check fails, increment a counter and retry.
11. If every check passes, publish the curve and the complete transcript.

Because the number of attempts is uncertain but every returned result is intended to satisfy deterministic checks, the design is described as a **Las Vegas algorithm** (Bhowmik, 2025). The randomness affects runtime, not the definition of an acceptable output.

---

## 6. Stage One: Fixing the Public Inputs

The transcript begins with

$$
(p,DS,\sigma).
$$

The components serve different purposes.

### The field prime $p$

The prime determines $\mathbb{F}_p$. A research prototype may use a small prime so that point counting can be performed naively. A deployment candidate would require a prime of an appropriate bit length and additional implementation-aware selection criteria.

### The domain separator $DS$

A domain-separation string identifies the protocol and version, such as `SelmerGen-v1`. It prevents identical seed material from being interpreted in the same way by unrelated procedures.

### The public seed $\sigma$

The seed initializes deterministic derivation. It is not a private key. Its origin and selection rule must be documented; otherwise, publishing it does not prevent unexplained seed shopping.

The proposal derives field elements by counter-based hashing:

$$
u_i=H(\text{label}\parallel DS\parallel\sigma\parallel\langle i\rangle)\bmod p.
$$

Separate labels—such as `F2`, `F3`, and `REC`—create independent logical streams for the quartic, cubic, and reconciliation stages. A reproducible specification must also fix the hash function, byte encoding, counter width, endianness, and reduction rule. The phrase “hash these values” is not sufficiently precise for independent reproduction.

---

## 7. Stage Two: Building the Binary Quartic

The 2-descent side begins with a binary quartic

$$
f(x,z)=ax^4+bx^3z+cx^2z^2+dxz^3+ez^4,
$$

whose five coefficients are derived from the `F2` stream.

For one common normalization, its classical invariants are

$$
I=12ae-3bd+c^2
$$

and

$$
J=72ace+9bcd-27ad^2-27b^2e-2c^3.
$$

These polynomial combinations have a crucial property: they behave predictably under changes of variables. They compress the form's coordinate-dependent coefficients into coordinate-aware algebraic data. The proposed construction normalizes them into a candidate pair $(c_4^{(2)},c_6^{(2)})$ (Bhowmik, 2025).

Normalization conventions vary across invariant-theory references. That is not cosmetic. Multiplying an invariant by a fixed factor changes later formulas unless every stage uses the same convention. A reproducible transcript must therefore name the formulas—not merely record symbols called $c_4$ and $c_6$.

The quartic is rejected if it is trivial, a perfect square, singular, or unable to pass the stated solubility proxy.

---

## 8. What the Local-Solubility Proxy Checks

In genuine descent, local solubility is examined over relevant completions of the base field. The proof-of-concept uses a simpler computational analogue. It asks whether

$$
y^2=f(x,1)
$$

has a solution over $\mathbb{F}_p$ and over a fixed collection of small finite fields, for example

$$
\mathbb{F}_2,\mathbb{F}_3,\mathbb{F}_5,\mathbb{F}_7,\mathbb{F}_{11}.
$$

At a small prime $\ell$, exhaustive testing is straightforward:

1. Evaluate $f(x,1)$ for every $x\in\mathbb{F}_\ell$.
2. Determine whether at least one value is a quadratic residue.
3. Reject the form if no solution appears.

At large $p$, bounded search can serve as a prototype filter, but failure to find a point within a bound is not a proof of nonsolvability. Conversely, finite-field checks are not equivalent to full $p$-adic solubility tests. The current method is therefore best described exactly as the paper describes it: a **local-solubility proxy modeled on Selmer admissibility**, not a full descent computation (Bhowmik, 2025).

That limitation belongs in the transcript and in any claim made about the output.

---

## 9. Stage Three: Building the Ternary Cubic

The 3-descent side constructs a homogeneous ternary cubic

$$
F(x,y,z)=\sum_{i+j+k=3}b_{ijk}x^iy^jz^k.
$$

There are 10 degree-three monomials in three variables, so 10 coefficients come from the independently labeled `F3` stream. The form is rejected if it is singular or fails the declared solubility tests.

Ternary cubics possess classical invariants, often expressed through Aronhold invariants and related modern normalizations. These yield another candidate pair

$$
(c_4^{(3)},c_6^{(3)}).
$$

The explicit formulas are substantially larger than the binary-quartic formulas. A trustworthy implementation should not hide them behind an unexplained function call. It should identify the exact normalization, provide test vectors, and cross-check results with an independent computer algebra system.

The proof-of-concept reported in the preprint uses placeholder ternary-cubic invariant mappings. Replacing them with the full invariant machinery is explicitly identified as future work (Bhowmik, 2025). That means the prototype demonstrates the architecture of the pipeline, not a finished production generator.

---

## 10. Stage Four: Deterministic Reconciliation

At this point there are two invariant pairs. The proposal combines them rather than selecting one branch.

First, it hashes the serialized pairs under reconciliation-specific labels:

$$
\widetilde{c}_4=
H(\mathtt{REC\_c4}\parallel DS\parallel\sigma\parallel c_4^{(2)}\parallel c_4^{(3)})
\bmod p,
$$

$$
\widetilde{c}_6=
H(\mathtt{REC\_c6}\parallel DS\parallel\sigma\parallel c_6^{(2)}\parallel c_6^{(3)})
\bmod p.
$$

It then forms the final candidate using fixed linear combinations:

$$
c_4=2c_4^{(2)}+3\widetilde{c}_4\pmod p,
$$

$$
c_6=2c_6^{(2)}+3\widetilde{c}_6\pmod p.
$$

The constants and labels are public protocol choices. Their purpose is not to make the curve more secure by secrecy; it is to make the combination rule unambiguous and reproducible.

This stage illustrates why serialization belongs to cryptographic mathematics. Two implementations that encode a field element with different byte orders will hash different strings and generate different curves, even if every displayed equation agrees.

---

## 11. Stage Five: Instantiating the Curve

For a general short-Weierstrass model

$$
E: y^2=x^3+Ax+B
$$

over a field of characteristic other than 2 or 3, the discriminant is

$$
\Delta=-16(4A^3+27B^2).
$$

The curve is nonsingular exactly when $\Delta\neq0$. A zero discriminant means the cubic has a repeated root and the geometric object is not an elliptic curve.

The Selmer-inspired proposal maps its reconciled invariants into the model

$$
E/\mathbb{F}_p:
y^2=x^3-27c_4x-54c_6
$$

and rejects a candidate if its discriminant vanishes (Bhowmik, 2025). Because multiple $c_4,c_6$ normalizations occur in the literature, an implementation should compute $A$ and $B$ first and then evaluate the standard discriminant formula above. That is easier to audit than relying on an unstated scaling identity.

Passing this test proves only that the curve is mathematically nonsingular. It says almost nothing about cryptographic suitability.

---

## 12. Stage Six: The Cryptographic Validation Gate

This stage carries the security burden. The ancestry of a candidate may be elegant, but the candidate must still pass ordinary ECC validation.

### 12.1 Count the points

Compute

$$
N=\#E(\mathbb{F}_p).
$$

Hasse's bound supplies a consistency interval:

$$
|N-(p+1)|\leq2\sqrt{p}.
$$

At tiny demonstration primes, one can enumerate all $x$ values and count square roots. At cryptographic sizes, point counting requires established algorithms and vetted implementations. Schoof (1985) gave the foundational deterministic polynomial-time algorithm; practical systems use extensions and optimizations such as Schoof–Elkies–Atkin.

### 12.2 Factor the group order

Write

$$
N=h\,r,
$$

where $r$ is the intended large prime subgroup order and $h$ is the cofactor. The target size of $r$ must correspond to the desired security level, and the cofactor must satisfy the protocol's explicit bound. NIST (2023) treats the subgroup order and cofactor as core domain parameters, not incidental metadata.

### 12.3 Select and verify a base point

A complete parameter set also needs a point $G$ of order $r$. Given a point $P$ on the curve, a common construction is

$$
G=hP.
$$

The verifier must check that $G$ is not the identity and that

$$
rG=\mathcal{O}.
$$

The transcript should specify how $P$ was derived so that the base point is reproducible too.

### 12.4 Check the quadratic twist

If the curve has trace

$$
t=p+1-N,
$$

its quadratic twist has order

$$
N'=p+1+t=2p+2-N.
$$

Factor $N'$ and require a suitably large prime factor with an acceptable twist cofactor. Twist security reduces the damage from implementations or protocols that process points not lying on the intended curve. It does not excuse skipping point validation; it adds another layer of robustness (Bernstein, 2006; Bos et al., 2016).

### 12.5 Reject anomalous curves

If

$$
N=p,
$$

the curve is anomalous. Smart (1999) showed that the discrete logarithm problem on trace-one curves can be solved efficiently. Such a candidate must be rejected.

### 12.6 Reject small embedding degrees

For prime subgroup order $r$, the embedding degree is the smallest positive integer $k$ such that

$$
r\mid p^k-1.
$$

If $k$ is small, pairings can transfer the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem into the multiplicative group of an extension field, where different algorithms apply. The MOV reduction established this risk (Menezes et al., 1993). A generator intended for ordinary, non-pairing ECC should reject unexpectedly small embedding degrees.

### 12.7 Screen for other special structure

The proposal also calls for rejecting pathological or deliberately structured cases, including small-discriminant complex multiplication cases under the chosen policy. The exact screen must be stated algorithmically. “No suspicious structure” is not a reproducible test.

## 13. Reading the Published Demonstration Transcript

The proof-of-concept uses the small prime

$$
p=100{,}003
$$

with domain separator `SelmerGen-v1` and a fixed public seed. It reports the following summary (Bhowmik, 2025):

| Parameter | Demonstration value |
|---|---:|
| $c_4$ | 82,765 |
| $c_6$ | 79,541 |
| Reported discriminant modulo $p$ | 53,954 |
| $\#E(\mathbb{F}_p)$ | $99{,}711=81\times1{,}231$ |
| Curve cofactor $h$ | 81 |
| Large factor $r$ | 1,231 |
| Twist order | 100,297, which is prime |
| Small embedding degree through $k=20$ | None detected |

Several independent consistency checks are immediately available.

First, the reported discriminant is nonzero, so the candidate passes the basic nonsingularity gate under the stated normalization.

Second, the curve and twist orders satisfy

$$
99{,}711+100{,}297=200{,}008=2p+2,
$$

which is exactly the expected curve–twist relationship.

Third, both reported orders lie within Hasse's interval around $p+1$.

The most important conclusion, however, is a limitation: **this demonstration curve is not a production-acceptable output under the paper's own target cofactor policy**. Its cofactor is 81 rather than one of the stated small target values, and its prime subgroup factor 1,231 is tiny. The example exercises transcript generation and validation calculations over a manageable field; it does not supply deployable security.

That distinction strengthens rather than weakens reproducibility. An audit trail should make rejection reasons visible. A useful prototype can demonstrate a method even when its toy output is correctly labeled as unsuitable for deployment.

## 14. What a Complete Audit Transcript Should Contain

Publishing only $(p,A,B)$ would lose most of the proposal's value. A complete transcript should include at least:

| Stage | Required record |
|---|---|
| Protocol | Version, hash, encodings, endianness, counters, labels |
| Inputs | $p$, $DS$, $\sigma$, and the rule that selected each |
| 2-descent branch | Quartic coefficients, invariant formulas, invariant values, solubility results |
| 3-descent branch | Cubic coefficients, invariant formulas, invariant values, solubility results |
| Reconciliation | Serialized hash inputs, hash outputs, final $c_4,c_6$ |
| Curve | $A,B,\Delta$ and the exact conversion formulas |
| Order checks | $N$, factorization, $r$, $h$, method and software version |
| Base point | Derivation of $P$, resulting $G$, and order checks |
| Twist checks | Twist definition, $N'$, factorization, $r'$, $h'$ |
| Structural checks | Anomalous, embedding-degree, CM, and policy results |
| Retry history | Every rejected counter and its first failing condition |

The retry history is essential. If a designer publishes only the successful attempt, observers cannot tell whether rejected candidates followed the public rule or whether private selection occurred between attempts.

A machine-readable transcript might use a structure like this:

```json
{
  "specification": "SelmerGen-v1",
  "field_prime": "100003",
  "seed": "0123456789abcdef...",
  "attempt": 42,
  "quartic": {
    "coefficients": ["a", "b", "c", "d", "e"],
    "normalization": "named-formula-version",
    "local_checks": {"2": true, "3": true, "5": true}
  },
  "ternary_cubic": {
    "coefficients": ["b300", "b210", "..."],
    "normalization": "named-formula-version"
  },
  "curve": {
    "A": "...",
    "B": "...",
    "discriminant": "53954",
    "order": "99711",
    "factorization": [["3", 4], ["1231", 1]]
  },
  "decision": {
    "accepted": false,
    "reason": "cofactor 81 exceeds policy"
  }
}
```

This is an illustrative schema, not a serialization standard. A real specification would use complete values, canonical encodings, and a schema version.

## 15. Auditor's Reproduction Procedure

An independent auditor should be able to perform the following steps without contacting the curve designer:

1. Parse the declared version and canonical input encodings.
2. Recompute every labeled hash stream from $(p,DS,\sigma)$.
3. Reconstruct the quartic and cubic coefficients.
4. Recompute all invariants using a separately implemented formula set.
5. Repeat every local-solubility proxy and compare witnesses.
6. Recompute the reconciliation hashes and final invariant pair.
7. Derive $A$ and $B$ and verify $\Delta\neq0$.
8. Count $\#E(\mathbb{F}_p)$ using trusted software.
9. Verify the factorization, subgroup order, cofactor, and base point.
10. Construct the twist and verify its order and factorization.
11. Repeat anomalous, embedding-degree, and structural checks.
12. Reproduce every rejected attempt before the accepted counter.
13. Confirm that the first policy-compliant candidate—not a later preferred candidate—was selected.

Using a second implementation matters. Re-running the author's code can establish determinism while preserving the same bug. Independent formulas and software provide stronger evidence.

## 16. Pseudocode With Failure Made Explicit

The core control flow can be expressed without pretending that the difficult algebraic routines are one-line operations:

```python
def generate_curve(spec, p, domain, seed, policy):
    for attempt in range(policy.max_attempts):
        streams = derive_labeled_streams(
            spec=spec,
            p=p,
            domain=domain,
            seed=seed,
            attempt=attempt,
        )

        quartic = derive_binary_quartic(streams["F2"])
        q2 = validate_quartic(quartic, p, policy.local_primes)
        if not q2.accepted:
            record_rejection(attempt, "quartic", q2.reason)
            continue
        c4_2, c6_2 = exact_quartic_invariants(quartic, spec)

        cubic = derive_ternary_cubic(streams["F3"])
        q3 = validate_cubic(cubic, p, policy.local_primes)
        if not q3.accepted:
            record_rejection(attempt, "cubic", q3.reason)
            continue
        c4_3, c6_3 = exact_cubic_invariants(cubic, spec)

        c4, c6 = reconcile(
            streams["REC"], c4_2, c6_2, c4_3, c6_3, p
        )
        curve = instantiate_curve(c4, c6, p, spec)

        validation = validate_for_cryptography(curve, policy)
        record_attempt(attempt, quartic, cubic, curve, validation)

        if validation.accepted:
            return curve, complete_transcript()

    raise RuntimeError("No acceptable curve within the attempt limit")
```

Functions such as `exact_cubic_invariants`, `count_points`, and `factor_group_order` are the hard parts. Giving them descriptive names is not an implementation. Each requires specified mathematics, test vectors, vetted libraries, and independent verification.

## 17. What the Method Contributes

The proposal's most interesting contribution is not a claim that descent makes the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem harder. It does not change that hardness assumption.

Instead, it treats **provenance as an output**:

- The quartic and cubic explain where candidate invariants entered the process.
- Solubility-inspired checks record arithmetic rejection criteria.
- Domain-separated hashing makes reconciliation deterministic.
- Conventional validation determines cryptographic acceptability.
- Retry records show that the stated selection rule was followed.

This is a different philosophy from generating coefficients directly from a random string. The process yields a mathematical family tree, not just a seed and final curve.

## 18. Open Problems Before Production Use

The preprint identifies several unresolved requirements (Bhowmik, 2025). They are substantial.

### Replace proxy invariants and solubility tests

The ternary-cubic stage needs complete, verified Aronhold–Dixmier invariant computations. Bounded searches should be replaced or supplemented by mathematically justified local-solubility procedures appropriate to the claimed construction.

### Clarify normalization end to end

Every invariant, discriminant, and curve-conversion formula must use a single explicit convention. Test vectors should cover intermediate values, not only final coefficients.

### Formalize the candidate distribution

The security filters can reject known weak structures, but the distribution induced by the quartic, cubic, and reconciliation stages is not yet formally characterized. Hidden bias would not automatically imply weakness, but it must be understood.

### Scale point counting and factorization

Cryptographic-size experiments need reproducible performance measurements using established computer algebra systems or verified libraries. The cost of retries must include point counting, twist counting, and order factorization.

### Define a complete acceptance policy

Terms such as “large prime,” “small cofactor,” “small CM discriminant,” and “unexpectedly low embedding degree” must become concrete numerical rules fixed before candidate generation.

### Subject the construction to independent cryptanalysis

Novel parameter-generation methods require adversarial review. Arithmetic structure is valuable for provenance only if it does not introduce exploitable structure into the resulting group. No generator should be treated as production-ready on the strength of its designer's analysis alone.

### Separate generation from implementation security

Even excellent parameters can be undermined by variable-time arithmetic, invalid-point handling, nonce failures, or protocol misuse. Curve provenance complements constant-time implementation and protocol validation; it does not replace them.

---

## Conclusion

The path from a Selmer-inspired form to a cryptographic curve has two distinct halves.

The first half is constructive and arithmetic: derive binary quartics and ternary cubics, compute invariants, apply solubility-inspired filters, and reconcile the outputs through an explicit deterministic rule.

The second half is eliminative and cryptographic: reject singular curves, weak group orders, large cofactors, weak twists, anomalous cases, low embedding degrees, and any other structure excluded by policy.

The second half decides whether a curve is an acceptable candidate. The first half explains where it came from.

That division is the central idea. Transparent provenance cannot prove security, but it can remove ambiguity from parameter selection. A complete transcript lets independent observers reproduce not merely the final curve, but the decisions that led to it—and the rejected candidates that did not.
